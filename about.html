About Autism

Accurate, respectful information about autism spectrum disorder — what it is, how it presents, and how to seek support.

Overview

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental difference that affects how people communicate, interact socially, and process sensory information. It is called a "spectrum" because it manifests uniquely in every individual — there is no single "autism experience."

Autism is not a disease or disorder to be "cured." Many autistic people describe autism as a core part of their identity. The goal of support and intervention is to improve quality of life and help each person thrive on their own terms.

Signs & Characteristics

💬

Communication differences

Delayed speech, preference for written over verbal communication, literal interpretation of language, or highly detailed verbal expression.

🤝

Social interaction

Difficulty reading social cues, preference for direct communication, challenges with unwritten social rules or small talk.

🔁

Repetitive behaviors

Stimming (self-stimulatory behavior), strong routines, deep focus on specific interests, need for consistency.

🌡️

Sensory sensitivities

Over- or under-sensitivity to sound, light, touch, taste, or smell. Sensory overload can be overwhelming.

Early Signs by Age

Early identification leads to significantly better outcomes. Here are signs to look for at each stage of development.

6 months

Limited social smiling or eye contact

Typical infants smile responsively and make eye contact. Limited response to name or faces may be an early indicator.

12 months

Limited babbling or gestures

May not wave, point, or babble to communicate. Does not respond consistently when name is called.

18 months

No single words

No single meaningful words spoken. Loss of previously acquired speech or social skills is a significant warning sign.

24 months

No two-word spontaneous phrases

Reduced shared attention, strong attachment to specific objects or routines, limited imitation of others.

Any age

Talk to your pediatrician

If you notice any of these signs, request a developmental screening. Early intervention — including speech therapy, occupational therapy, and educational support — significantly improves outcomes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Research shows autism results from a combination of genetic and environmental factors. There is no single known cause. Vaccines do not cause autism — this has been thoroughly debunked by large-scale international studies. No parenting style or food causes autism.

Many autistic people and organizations use identity-first language ("autistic person") and describe autism as a core part of who they are. Others prefer person-first language ("person with autism"). Follow the individual's preference.

Common supports include speech-language therapy, occupational therapy, social skills groups, and educational accommodations (IEPs). Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) is widely used but is debated within the autistic community — quality varies widely. The right support depends on the individual's specific needs and preferences. Always involve the autistic person in decisions about their care.
No — intelligence varies widely across the autism spectrum, just as it does in the general population. Many autistic individuals have exceptional abilities in specific areas (mathematics, music, memory, pattern recognition). Co-occurring intellectual disabilities affect some autistic people but are not inherent to autism.
Diagnosis involves a comprehensive evaluation by trained clinicians including developmental pediatricians, child psychologists, or psychiatrists. There is no blood test or brain scan for autism. Evaluations assess developmental history, behavior observations, and standardized assessments (such as ADOS-2 and ADI-R). If you suspect autism in your child or yourself, speak with your primary care physician for a referral.
